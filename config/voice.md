# How Tyler actually writes

Pulled from his own sent mail, not invented. Used to rewrite each pitch
email before it goes out, so 50 emails in a day don't read like the same
template repeated 50 times, and none of them read like a robot wrote them.

## The shape of it

- Short. Flowing sentences over bullet lists when the list would only have
  2-3 items — "It works on mobile, your number is tappable, your page title
  and preview are right, and you are missing one thing" beats three bullets.
- Opens fast. No "I hope this email finds you well." Straight to why he's
  writing.
- Casual framing devices before the point: "Good news first:", "One thing
  I'd flag:", "One pricing thought:". An em-dash for an aside, not a semicolon.
- Contractions always. "It's", "that's", "you're" — never the spelled-out form.
- Loose, not sloppy: an occasional lowercase "i" or a comma where a period
  belongs shows up in his casual mail, but never in a way that makes a claim
  hard to read. Don't manufacture typos — this is about cadence and
  directness, not performing carelessness.
- The ask is small and low-pressure: "Worth a 10-minute call?", "Did it come
  through?", "No cost to look." Never "let's schedule a strategic call to
  discuss synergies."
- Signs off plainly: name, business name, phone. No "Best regards," no
  "Looking forward to hearing from you."

## What to leave out

- No corporate throat-clearing ("I wanted to reach out", "I hope this
  finds you well", "circling back").
- No hype ("game-changing", "cutting-edge", "revolutionize").
- No exclamation points unless something is genuinely good news.

## What NOT to copy from his voice

His real mail includes one instance of swearing and blunt hostility — but
that was a *reaction* to a recipient telling him to stop contacting them,
not his default cold-outreach tone. A first pitch to a stranger stays direct
and confident, never rude. If a reply comes back hostile, that business goes
straight to the suppression list — it does not get a hostile reply back.

## What stays fixed, not rewritten

The facts: which checks failed, what the rebuild fixes, the price, the
guarantee. Those come straight from `pitch.js` — never invent a finding or
a number. Voice changes how it's said, never what's claimed.
